Green Goods: From Good Intentions to Green Outcomes

Green Goods gives a community one record of what it promised, did and proved, and helps money from people and programs who already care reach it. Organizations pay for the service; communities and individual supporters never pay a fee. Every figure here is illustrative.

Key partners

  • Tech and Sun: solar-powered hubs in Nigeria; the pilot garden, at its Awka hub.
  • Omo Yoruba of Southern California: the first diaspora community.
  • Grassroots Economics: service vouchers on Cosmo-Local Credit.
  • GoodDollar for G$ rewards; Octant and Aave for endowments.
  • Hypercerts: impact certificates; Hypercerts Certified: publishing to Ma Earth.
  • Bloom Network and Regen Network: where the record counts next.
  • Open infrastructure: attestations (EAS), Hats Protocol, Safe, Chainlink CCIP, Envio, IPFS.
  • Greenpill Network: chapters and guilds that bring gardens.

Key activities

  • Onboard gardens and train their stewards.
  • Run seasons of promises; review the evidence.
  • Report to funders in a form they can act on.
  • Build and run the apps, contracts and indexer.
  • Connect partner networks, so one record counts everywhere.

Key resources

  • The record: signed attestations, read by our indexer.
  • The apps: member, steward, chat and public pages.
  • Contracts: gardens, promises, rewards, vouchers, endowments.
  • Passkey accounts, sponsored fees.
  • A small team in the Greenpill network.

Value propositions

  • For communities: one record of what they promised, did and proved, owned by the garden and usable offline, since captured work waits on the phone until back online; a lower cost to report impact: a tap and a photo, or a chat message; promises between members with agreed rewards; credibility members carry to the next employer, mentor or funder.
  • For funders: delivery they can see before and after they give, confirmed by the people served (or, as a labeled fallback, a steward), never by the person who did the work; reporting they can act on, at a lower cost; more local initiatives reached.
  • For the diaspora: a way to support home with evidence, not only transfers, alone or through an association that pools members' gifts.
  • For networks and registries: report once, and let the record count everywhere.

Customer relationships

  • Hands-on setup of each garden with its stewards, then support through the season.
  • Evaluators set the baseline and what good evidence looks like.
  • Funders get reporting and a place in the record.
  • Community members confirm what was done for them.
  • Public pages anyone can check.

Channels

  • The Green Goods app, installable and offline-ready, in English, Spanish and Portuguese.
  • Chat reporting by Telegram.
  • Public garden pages at greengoods.app.
  • The steward dashboard.
  • Partner networks that carry the record.
  • Greenpill chapters and guilds.

Customer segments

  • Place-based communities doing regenerative work: solar hubs, agroforestry collectives, waste cleanups, education programs. Pilot: Tech and Sun's hub in Awka; South Africa next; Kenya and Brazil exploring.
  • Funders of that work: climate, clean energy and skills programs, foundations, public goods funds, and institutions piloting allocation tools.
  • Diaspora supporters and their associations, starting with the Nigerian diaspora.
  • Networks and registries that need evidence behind claims.

Who pays: funders, programs and associations. Who uses: gardeners, stewards, evaluators, funders and community members.

Cost structure Illustrative USD

Delivery for one six-month program, priced by hours. Central costs (product, sales and administration) sit outside this figure.

Costs scale with gardens and seasons, not with the funds that move. Setup comes once per garden, so later seasons cost less.

  • Steward training and support, 50 h$2,000
  • Evidence review and reporting, 30 h$1,500

Revenue streams Illustrative USD

Fees for service. Programs, foundations, associations and NGOs pay a separate fee for setting a garden up, supporting its stewards through a season, and reporting the funder can act on, priced by projects and workload.

A share of endowment earnings. Endowments earn DeFi yield on ETH and stablecoins, typically 2 to 7% a year and not guaranteed; a 2.7% default share goes to Green Goods once switched on.

Planned: report and certificate fees once buying certificates ships. Today's income is grants and ecosystem funds. Never a supporter's deposit, never a garden's shared fund.
