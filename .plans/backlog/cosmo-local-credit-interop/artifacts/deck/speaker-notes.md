## 1. Title · 0:25

Green Goods helps communities turn good intentions into green outcomes. We built commitment pooling: members promise useful work, prove it, and turn the record into support. Today I'll show it at Tech and Sun's solar hub in Awka, Nigeria, and show how diaspora and institutional capital can follow that record and sustain the work.

## 2. The problem · 0:30

Communities create value every season: repairs, workshops, plantings, mentoring. But the record of that work stays trapped. Proving the work costs more than doing it. Reporting tools were built for offices, not the field. And the record sits on platforms the community doesn't own. Inside the community, contribution is thanked but not valued. Outside, supporters can't read it, so groups with a real delivery record still look risky and stay tied to single grants.

## 3. The solution · 0:25

So we built Green Goods. It gives a community one record of what it promised, did and proved. We call each community a garden. Gardeners do the work and document it, by app or by chat, and stewards run the garden. The garden owns its record, and supporters can read it and fund what it shows.

## 4. Five roles · 0:15

Five roles keep a garden going. Gardeners do and document the work. Stewards run the garden. Evaluators set the baseline. Funders see delivery before and after they give. And community members confirm what was done for them.

## 5. The loop · 0:30

A garden runs as a loop, season after season. Needs: stewards and evaluators record what the place needs and where things stand today. Promise: members promise work to each other and to the garden, and agree who does what by when. Work: it is documented with photos and notes, reviewed by stewards and confirmed by the people it served. Funding: supporters fund what the record shows. And what was learned shapes the next season's needs.

## 6. Commitment pooling · 0:35

We built commitment pooling for that Promise step. Members offer or request specific work, like a workshop, a repair or a planting, and see what others offer and need, so skills and help move between them. Anyone in the garden can take a promise up. When the work is done, the person it was made for confirms it was kept. The person who did it never can, and if a steward has to step in, the record shows it. These are real app screens with demo data.

## 7. Cosmo-Local vouchers · 0:30

For this hackathon we connected promises to Cosmo-Local service vouchers, the open protocol from Grassroots Economics. A member or a garden offers a service as vouchers: an hour of help, a hub visit, a career call. Kept promises can earn vouchers, and members can swap them in one shared pool. Booking holds the vouchers in escrow until the service happens. Then delivered units go back to the issuer, and anything undelivered returns to the holder.

## 8. Tech and Sun · 0:30

We're starting with Tech and Sun, an initiative that builds solar-powered hubs in Nigeria. Its first hub is live in Awka, near Nnamdi Azikiwe University, and its plan names Enugu and Abuja next. Members told us internet and power come first, then learning, engineering and AI. In an October survey, introductions to work and one-to-one career calls came out on top. This is the hub's live garden page, and its counts come from the record.

## 9. Member use cases · 0:35

So what do members get? Credibility they can carry. In the pilot we're setting up, a designer, an analyst and an engineer build a website together, and each confirmed part goes into their portfolio. Vouchers earned on projects can book career calls and one-to-one help, which is what members asked for. Vouchers can also open hub visits and AI tool seats members can't afford alone. Because the person who did the work never confirms it, the record carries weight with the next employer, mentor or funder.

## 10. Four levels of capital · 0:20

Hubs are rich in capital, but short of money. Around the hub, the community adds social, cultural, material and living capital. The diaspora brings money, culture, relationships and expertise, and institutions bring money, knowledge and equipment. Money flows in, and evidence of delivery radiates back out.

## 11. Sources of capital · 0:25

That money can come from people and programs who already care. Diaspora supporters give gifts and sponsor seasons. Diaspora associations, like Omo Yoruba, can commission a whole program themselves, with no institution needed. Institutions and programs fund climate, clean energy and skills. And public goods funds in our ecosystem already back Green Goods.

## 12. The Nigerian diaspora · 0:30

The Nigerian diaspora sent 21.3 billion dollars home in 2024, according to the African Development Bank. That is national context, not our market. The diaspora also brings culture and connections. Our first diaspora community is Omo Yoruba of Southern California, a Los Angeles nonprofit since 1997. Here they are at a festival and in a Yoruba language class. Other diaspora associations can follow the same path.

## 13. Three mechanisms · 0:35

Three mechanisms turn that support into lasting capacity. First, the endowment: supporters deposit, the capital stays invested, and its earnings, which vary, go to the garden. Neither principal nor earnings is guaranteed. Second, the commitment pool, which is live: members promise work, and kept promises earn rewards and build the record. Third, the exchange pool, in pilot: members trade vouchers in one shared pool. Next, supporters and endowment earnings could add funds that members exchange vouchers into. That step is future work, not a cash-out promise.

## 14. Business model · 0:30

How does Green Goods sustain itself? Funders pay us to help them reach more local initiatives. A funder sends money to the garden for the work, and pays Green Goods a separate fee for setup, support and reporting they can act on. We price by projects and workload, never as a cut of a garden's funds or endowment capital. Individual supporters never need a subscription. A worked example is in our companion brief.

## 15. Where we are and where next · 0:30

Where are we today? The app is live, and promises and chat reporting joined it in October. We start with one hub in Nigeria, where the first promise season is planned to run into January. Then more places on the same model: waste work in South Africa next, and agroforestry in Kenya and Brazil, which we are exploring with local groups.

## 16. Technical roadmap · 0:20

Our technical goal is simple: report once, and let the record count everywhere. Communities report by app or by chat, and Green Goods carries that record to networks they already use: Cosmo-Local Credit now, Ma Earth next, then Bloom Network. With Regen Network we are exploring evidence for ecological claims.

## 17. What we need · 0:25

Beyond funding, here is what would help us most: business development mentorship; introductions to institutional and diaspora funders who want to pilot allocation tools like Green Goods; and marketing support to help us tell our story. So help us find the first supporter, and tell us what would make you the second.

## Business model reference for Q&A · illustrative USD assumptions

This six-month Tech and Sun program shows how the business could work. It is **not a quote,
secured funding, a validated price, or new implementation scope**. The first ask remains one
cohort, priced against agreed participants, capacity and deliverables.

| Illustrative contributor | Garden allocation | Separate Green Goods fee | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Environmental or clean-energy program | $30,000 | $10,000 | $40,000 |
| Diaspora: 100 supporters × $100 | $10,000 | $0 | $10,000 |
| **Total** | **$40,000** | **$10,000** | **$50,000** |

A diaspora association, NGO, foundation, community network or program organizer could be the
paying customer. An individual supporter never needs to subscribe. Diaspora-led funding can
proceed without an institutional sponsor, and institutional support needs no diaspora match.
Garden allocations reach the approved Tech and Sun recipient. Confirm recipient, gift method,
terms and fees before collecting anything. Voucher face value is not extra cash raised.

| Garden use over six months | Assumed budget |
|---|---:|
| Participant stipends and project delivery | $12,000 |
| Local stewardship and facilitation | $8,000 |
| Hub power, connectivity, maintenance and tools | $8,000 |
| Learning, mentoring and approved service access | $8,000 |
| Contingency, transaction costs and remedies | $4,000 |
| **Total garden allocation** | **$40,000** |

Proposed fee: **$3,000 setup + $7,000 for six months of support and reporting = $10,000**. It buys
a defined service, not a percentage of a garden's funds or endowment capital. No asset-based
charge, swap-fee income or certificate revenue is assumed.

| Direct delivery assumption | Calculation | Cost |
|---|---:|---:|
| Setup and onboarding | 25 hours × $50 | $1,250 |
| Steward training and support | 50 hours × $40 | $2,000 |
| Evidence review and reporting | 30 hours × $50 | $1,500 |
| Technical operations | 15 hours × $60 | $900 |
| Hosting, messaging and sponsored transactions | Six-month allowance | $350 |
| Delivery contingency | Allowance | $500 |
| **Total** | **120 labor hours + allowances** | **$6,500** |

**Contribution: $10,000 − $6,500 = $3,500, or 35%,** before central sales, product development and
administration, so it is not net profit. If support needs another 50 hours at $40, cost rises to
$8,500 and contribution falls to **$1,500, or 15%**. Measure delivery hours, willingness to pay
and renewal before claiming repeatable margins.

## Endowment reference for Q&A · existing foundation, future integration

The endowment predates this hackathon. A depositor keeps a withdrawal claim; the funds are lent
through an external market, and actual earnings go to configured destinations. Principal can
lose value, earnings vary and withdrawals depend on available liquidity. The code's default split
is 48.65% to the garden's shared fund, 48.65% toward certificate fractions and 2.7% to protocol
backing; that is not a read of Tech and Sun's configuration and not Green Goods revenue. No route
today sends earnings or principal into the exchange pool or a milestone grant.

**Optional capital illustration.** Add a separate **$50,000 endowment** to the **$50,000
program** and the total is **$100,000**, but only $50,000 is budgeted for spending: $40,000 for
the garden and $10,000 for Green Goods. The other $50,000 stays invested under the depositor's
claim. At a **hypothetical 4% annual gross rate**, $50,000 × 4% × 6/12 = **$1,000 over six
months**, before costs, losses and the allocation. That is arithmetic, not an offered rate. If
the depositor later chooses to withdraw and grant $20,000, the same $100,000 becomes **$60,000
to the garden + $10,000 Green Goods fee + $30,000 retained capital**, ignoring earnings, losses
and costs. Never count the same principal as both retained and spent.

**Certificates and scale.** Certificate registration and minting exist; buying or claiming
fractions in the app is unfinished, and buyer demand is unproven. A certificate documents a
share of an impact claim, not equity, equipment ownership, an offset or a financial return. The
direction is repeatable five-figure to low-six-figure programs first; million-dollar portfolios
are a longer-term ambition, not a forecast.
