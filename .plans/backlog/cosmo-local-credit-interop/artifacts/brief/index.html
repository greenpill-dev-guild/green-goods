Green Goods: From Good Intentions to Green Outcomes

We built commitment pooling: communities promise useful work, prove it, and turn the record into support. The people a promise served confirm it was kept, never the people who did the work. We are starting with Tech and Sun, which builds solar-powered hubs in Nigeria; its first hub is live in Awka. We are also showing how diaspora and institutional capital can follow that record and sustain the work.

The problem

Communities create value every season, but the record of it stays trapped. Proving work costs more than doing it, reporting tools were built for offices, and the record sits on platforms communities don't own. Groups with a real delivery record still look risky and stay tied to single grants.

What we built

Commitment pooling: members offer or request specific work, see what others can offer and need, take promises up and document them. For this hackathon we connected promises to Cosmo-Local Credit service vouchers, so kept promises can earn services that members trade in one shared pool.

How it pays for itself

Funders pay Green Goods a separate fee for setup, support and reporting, priced by projects and workload. Green Goods' share of what each garden's endowment earns is 2.7% by default, set per garden; routing it to Green Goods is being switched on. Never a supporter's deposit, never a garden's shared fund. Illustrative six-month program: $50,000, with $40,000 to the garden and a $10,000 fee against $6,500 of delivery cost.

Where it stands

Live: the app, promises, chat reporting and endowments. Deployed: the voucher contracts, on public networks since 24 October. In pilot: service vouchers with Tech and Sun. Open now: the hub's first promise season, running to 31 January 2027. Next: the pilot's first voucher services, then more places on the same model.

What we need, beyond funding

  • Introductions to institutional and diaspora funders who want to pilot allocation tools like Green Goods.
  • Business development mentorship.
  • Marketing support to help tell our story.

How Green Goods works

Green Goods gives a community one record of what it promised, did and proved. It is an offline-ready app for place-based communities, which it calls gardens: gardeners do and document the work, and stewards run the garden from a steward dashboard. Each step leaves a record the garden owns and supporters can read.

  1. Needs. Stewards and evaluators record what the place needs, from steady power to new skills, and set a baseline.
  2. Promise. Members promise work to each other and to the garden, and agree who does what by when.
  3. Work. Gardeners document it with photos, notes, links or audio, by app or by chat. Stewards review it, and the people it served confirm it.
  4. Funding. Supporters fund what the record shows, directly or through an endowment. What was learned shapes the next season's needs.

Five roles

GardenersDo the work and document it, offline when they need to.
StewardsRun the garden: plan seasons, welcome members, review work and help members set up services.
EvaluatorsSet the baseline and what good evidence looks like, and record assessments.
FundersFund gardens directly or through endowments, and see delivery before and after they give.
Community membersWitness the work and confirm what was done for them.

Two rules hold throughout. The person who did the work never confirms it: the person served does, and if a steward has to step in, the record labels it a fallback. And a steward can help a member set up a service but cannot approve the member's obligations for them.

Commitment pooling in practice

  • A garden opens a season or campaign and sets its promise rules.
  • Members offer or request specific work. Anyone in the garden can take a promise up, as themselves or for their garden.
  • Members see what others nearby can offer and need, so skills, time and equipment move between them.
  • Proof is added and reviewed. The person the promise was made to confirms it was kept.
  • Kept promises earn the rewards the garden agreed, in G$ or service vouchers, and the record becomes evidence a funder can act on.

What the record holds

Work, approvals and assessments are signed attestations, so anyone can check who recorded what and when. Each promise keeps a permanent identity that cannot be transferred.

Service vouchers and the shared pool

Members trade services with vouchers, like an hour of help for a career call, and kept promises can earn them. A voucher represents its issuer's future service under published terms. It runs on Cosmo-Local Credit, the open voucher protocol from Grassroots Economics, which grew from the Sarafu community pools in Kenya.

Issue

A member or a garden offers a service as vouchers, like an hour of help or a hub visit.

Earn or exchange

Kept promises earn vouchers. Members can also swap them in one shared pool.

Book

Both sides consent, and the vouchers are held until the service happens.

Confirm

Delivered units go back to the issuer. Anything undelivered returns to the holder.

Who issues vouchers

  • Members. A member's own account owns their voucher and its reserve. A steward can help set it up, but only the member approves the exact service, terms and capacity; that consent step is still being built.
  • Gardens. A garden offers collective services through its existing issuer authority. A member may deliver on its behalf.
  • One shared pool. Member and garden vouchers trade in one curated pool. Its administrators set the assets, rates, fees and holding caps. Admission does not guarantee demand, delivery or cash.

How a booking stays fair

  • Exact consent from the holder and the issuer comes before acceptance.
  • Vouchers are held from booking until the service is accepted.
  • One final split: delivered units to the issuer, undelivered units to the holder.
  • A swap changes who holds a voucher, never the delivery record. Returned vouchers do not restore capacity already used.
  • No cash-out, automatic burn or pooled guarantee is promised.

Two kinds of reward

A promise can carry two kinds of reward: money in G$, GoodDollar's token on Celo, and service vouchers. G$ also lets members buy services offered in other gardens. This season's promises at Tech and Sun carry G$ rewards; service vouchers join them as the pilot's first services are agreed. Each offer uses one payment route: there is no bridge between G$ and vouchers, and no mixed checkout.

The Tech and Sun pilot

A designer, an analyst and an engineer share one brief for the hub's impact website, each with a separate promise, deliverable and reward. In one selected exchange, a member trades their service voucher for another member's, then books it. Peer help stays separate from the funded project work.

Evidence so far · 25 October 2026

The whole voucher flow, from earning and swapping to booking, delivery and refunds, has been rehearsed end to end with test accounts on private copies of the networks, including what happens when a step fails partway. The contracts are now deployed on the public networks and switched on, and each issuer holds its first twenty voucher units. No member has used one yet, and real participants, services, capacity and prices are still to be agreed.

Architecture

Green Goods runs on three public networks, each doing one job. Authenticated Chainlink CCIP messages coordinate them: they authorize actions on the other chain, but they never carry money or tokens across.

Around the contracts

  • Accounts. Passkey smart accounts: members sign with a fingerprint or face, and network fees are sponsored. A new profile keeps one address on all three networks.
  • Apps. An offline-first app for gardeners and members, a dashboard for stewards and a Telegram bot for chat reporting.
  • Data. An Envio indexer reads Arbitrum One and Celo today, with Gnosis next, so public garden pages load quickly.

Safety by design

  • Payment commands between networks carry a stable identity, so a retry never repeats one; voucher recovery is still being built.
  • Token ownership, pool administration and upgrade authority are separate and explicit.
  • Contracts can pause; upgrades need owner approval and compatibility checks.
  • Delegated or recovery authority over a member's vouchers needs that member's approval; the consent step is still being built.

Capital and the business model

Hubs like Tech and Sun's are rich in capital, but short of money. Green Goods helps money from people and programs who already care reach them, and shows delivery in return.

WhoBringsHow

Endowment · live

A supporter deposits DAI or WETH into a garden vault and keeps a withdrawal claim. Earnings split by default: 48.65% to the garden's shared fund, 48.65% to certificates, 2.7% for the protocol, whose routing to Green Goods is being switched on. Principal can lose value.

Commitment pool · live

Members promise work. Kept promises, confirmed by the people served (or a steward, as a labeled fallback), earn the agreed rewards in G$ or vouchers and build the record funders read.

Exchange pool · in pilot

Members swap service vouchers in one shared pool, like an hour of help for a career call. Later, supporters and endowment earnings could add funds to it; that route does not exist yet and is no cash-out promise.

Impact certificates · partly built

A garden turns a season's impact into a certificate, and part of the endowment's earnings is set aside for them. Gardens can mint them today; next, supporters can buy a share. Demand is unproven.

How Green Goods earns Illustrative USD assumptions

Green Goods earns in three ways. Fees for service: diaspora associations, NGOs, foundations and program organizers pay a separate fee to set a garden up, support its stewards through a season, and report in a form the funder can act on, priced by projects and workload. A share of endowment earnings: 2.7% of what each garden's endowment earns by default, set per garden; routing to Green Goods is being switched on. Planned: impact report and certificate fees once buying certificates ships. Today's income is grants and ecosystem funds. Never a supporter's deposit, never a garden's shared fund. Terms

Garden
A place-based community using Green Goods, with its own members, roles and record.
Promise
An offer or request for specific work, with terms, proof and a confirmation.
Commitment pool
A garden's set of open promises for a season or campaign.
Service voucher
A transferable entitlement to an issuer's future service, under published terms.
Exchange pool
The one shared pool where member and garden vouchers trade.
Endowment
A deposit that stays invested; its earnings are split between the garden, certificates and the protocol. Not guaranteed.
G$
GoodDollar's token on Celo, used for rewards and purchases.
Impact certificate
A documented share of an impact claim a garden registers; not equity, land, an offset or audited impact.
Attestation
A signed, public record of who stated what and when.
